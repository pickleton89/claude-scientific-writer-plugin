# Computable Quality & Credibility Metrics for Figures: A Candidate Framework Grounded in the 2026 "Chart Slop" Discourse

## TL;DR
- **A defensible automated figure-quality pipeline should score three orthogonal axes — graphical integrity (is it lying?), design quality/data-ink economy (is it cluttered or over-ornamented?), and generative-artifact/credibility signals (does it bear the fingerprints of LLM or diffusion generation?) — and the strongest 2026 evidence is that rule-based "linters" on extracted chart metadata reliably catch integrity violations, while LLM-as-judge panels and computer-vision segmentation handle the perceptual/aesthetic axes.**
- **The 2026 literature confirms a clean split in AI-figure failure modes: pixel/diffusion generators (Nano Banana, GPT-image) produce garbled text, distorted geometry, and hallucinated labels detectable by OCR-consistency and forensic CV; code-generating LLMs (Claude, GPT, Qwen-VL) instead produce execution errors, overlapping subplots, and "data-mapping errors" (series on the wrong axes) detectable by code-execution checks and image↔data cross-validation.**
- **Recommendation: build a tiered scorer — Tier 1 deterministic rule checks (axis truncation, aspect-ratio distortion, default-palette detection, OCR legibility), Tier 2 CV/segmentation metrics (data-ink ratio, gridline density, color entropy), Tier 3 a calibrated multimodal LLM-as-judge panel for chart-type appropriateness and semantic coherence — and validate each tier against human expert ratings before trusting any composite score.**

## Key Findings

The phrase "chart slop" sits at the intersection of two literatures that, as of 2026, are converging. One is the decades-old visualization-quality canon (Tufte; Cleveland & McGill; the Draco constraint formalism; the Behrisch *Quality Metrics for Information Visualization* survey). The other is the new, fast-moving body of work on detecting AI-generated and misleading visualizations. The good news for a pipeline-builder is that most of the abstract principles the first literature articulated qualitatively have, by 2026, been operationalized into computable checks by the second.

**The "AI slop" concept now has a formal academic grounding (HIGH confidence).** Kommers et al., "Why Slop Matters" (ACM AI Letters / arXiv:2601.06060, January 2026), argue slop resists strict definition and instead propose three Wittgensteinian family-resemblance properties: *superficial competence* ("a veneer of quality belied by a deeper lack of substance"), *asymmetric effort*, and *mass producibility*, varying along three dimensions (instrumental utility, personalization, surrealism). They note slop is "characterized by overtly 'idealized' output, flattening out individual variation into canonical patterns" — which for charts maps directly onto generic templated styling and default palettes. The complementary empirical paper, Shaib et al., "Measuring AI 'Slop' in Text" (arXiv:2509.19163, updated January 2026), shows binary slop judgments are subjective but correlate with latent dimensions like coherence and relevance, and operationalizes computable proxies (template rate, templates-per-token, repeated n-grams). The conceptual lesson transfers: *slop is best measured as a composite of interpretable sub-dimensions, not a single binary classifier.*

**Foundational principles that are directly computable (HIGH confidence).** Tufte's data-ink ratio (data-ink / total ink, ideal → 1.0) is explicitly pixel-based and therefore directly estimable from a rasterized figure via segmentation; chartjunk is "ink that does not tell the viewer anything new." The lie factor (= size of effect shown in graphic / size of effect in data; honest ≈ 1.0) is computable wherever data values can be recovered. Tufte's canonical example — the 1983 *New York Times* fuel-economy chart — has a lie factor of 14.8: the fuel-economy standard rose from 18 to 27.5 mpg (a 53% change) but was drawn as a line growing from 0.6″ to 5.3″ (a 783% change), and 783/53 = 14.8 (*The Visual Display of Quantitative Information*, p. 57). Cleveland & McGill (1984, *JASA* 79:531–554) rank ten elementary perceptual tasks by accuracy — "1. Position along a common scale; 2. Positions along nonaligned scales; 3. Length, direction, angle; 4. Area; 5. Volume, curvature; 6. Shading, colour saturation" — giving a principled, citable basis for scoring chart-type appropriateness automatically.

**Misleading-chart detection is now a benchmarked CV/NLP task (HIGH confidence).** Tonglet et al., "Is this chart lying to me?" (arXiv:2508.21675, ACL 2026), introduce Misviz (2,604 real-world charts annotated with 12 misleader types) and Misviz-synth (57,665 synthetic Matplotlib charts). Critically, they build a **rule-based linter operating on extracted axis metadata** that deterministically detects: truncated axis (does not start at zero), inverted axis, dual axis, inconsistent tick intervals, inconsistent binning size, and inappropriate item order. They explicitly note that misleaders requiring visual/contextual interpretation (misrepresentation, inappropriate axis ranges) are *not* covered by the linter — a key boundary for any pipeline. Their broader misleader set includes misrepresentation (visual encoding sizes don't match value labels) and 3D effects (distorting encoded sizes). Related work shows vision-language models are themselves easily fooled by these same manipulations — axis inversion affected all 10 models tested, aspect-ratio distortion 9, truncation 7 — which means an LLM-as-judge must be *explicitly instrumented* to check for these rather than trusted to notice them.

**AI-generated charts have two distinct failure signatures depending on the generator (HIGH confidence).** This is the single most important finding for credibility detection:

- *Pixel/diffusion generators* (Nano Banana, GPT-image, Midjourney): produce garbled/scrambled text because diffusion models "treat text as visual patterns rather than linguistic symbols," plus meaningless symbols, distorted geometry, impossible/floating elements, edge artifacts, and texture/resolution inconsistencies. SciFigDetect (Hu et al., arXiv:2604.08211, April 2026) is the first benchmark for AI-generated *scientific figure* detection (72,965 real / 150,807 synthetic figures across illustrations, overviews, and experimental figures), and its central finding is sobering: current detectors "fail dramatically in zero-shot transfer, exhibit strong generator-specific overfitting, and remain fragile under common post-processing corruptions." Forensic CNN-style detection alone is therefore not yet reliable.
- *Code-generating LLMs* (Claude, GPT, Qwen-VL via Matplotlib): RealChart2Code (Zhang et al., arXiv:2603.25804, March 2026; 2,896 instances across Replication/Reproduction/Refinement tasks) reports four primary error types — Syntax/Execution Errors, Layout/Structural Failures, Data Mapping Errors, and Instruction Neglect. Open-weight models "frequently hallucinate non-existent libraries or invoke invalid functions" and produce "overlapping subplots or incorrect grid definitions"; proprietary models instead produce *Data Mapping Errors* where "the visual structure is correct, but specific data series are mapped to the wrong axes." They also document "regressive editing": fixing one requested change inadvertently breaks previously correct code. Per The Decoder's RealChart2Code coverage, "Qwen3-VL-235B... spits out invalid API calls, like a nonexistent Matplotlib style parameter, in roughly 20 percent of cases." The complexity gap is stark: Qwen3-VL-235B and Intern-VL-3.5-241B scored just 3.6 and 3.4 (under half the leading proprietary models, with Claude-4.5-Opus at 8.2), and DeepSeek-VL-7B's generated code failed to run in over 90% of Chart Replication cases.

**Automated visualization-quality evaluation frameworks already exist and name their dimensions (HIGH confidence).** VisEval (Chen et al., arXiv:2407.00981, IEEE VIS 2024) scores generated visualizations on three automated dimensions — *validity* (does the code render a chart at all), *legality* (does the chart comply with the query and chart-type rules), and *readability* (is it legible — no overlap, no text spilling off-canvas). VisEval's readability evaluator is empirically validated: it "obtains an impressive SRCC score of 0.843" against human experts, exceeding the 0.782 inter-expert agreement — meaning an automated legibility/overlap/off-canvas check can match or beat human consistency. LIDA uses Visualization Error Rate (VER, % code-compilation failures) and Self-Evaluated Visualization Quality (SEVQ, a GPT-4 judge averaging six dimensions: code accuracy, data transformation, goal compliance, visualization type, data encoding, aesthetics). Draco encodes visualization design knowledge as hard/soft logical constraints solvable by a constraint solver — effectively a "visualization spell-checker" — and can learn soft-constraint weights from graphical-perception experiments. Google's PaperBanana scientific-figure agent (Google Research, arXiv:2601.23265) reports 45.8% content fidelity (below the 50% human reference) yet won a blind human evaluation handily: "the average win / tie / loss rate of PaperBanana from 3 human judges is 72.7% / 20.7% / 6.6%." This divergence between fidelity and perceived quality is itself a cautionary datapoint — perceived polish ("superficial competence") and substantive correctness must be scored separately.

## Details: A Candidate Metrics Framework

What follows is a structured, computable framework organized into five dimensions. For each metric I give a computable definition, what it detects, how to score it, and a confidence/provenance label distinguishing claims grounded directly in the literature from my reasoned extensions.

### Dimension 1 — Graphical Integrity (Is the figure lying?)

This dimension is the most directly automatable and has the strongest 2026 evidence base (the Misviz linter). These are largely deterministic rule checks operating on extracted axis/encoding metadata.

**Axis-truncation detection.** *Definition:* flag any quantitative axis (especially for bar charts) whose origin ≠ 0. *Detects:* the single most common deception. *Scoring:* binary flag plus a severity score = (suppressed range / full range). *Grounded directly in Misviz linter rules (HIGH).*

**Lie-factor estimation.** *Definition:* where underlying values are recoverable (OCR of data labels, or programmatic extraction via a chart-understanding model), compute (graphical magnitude ratio / data magnitude ratio); honest ≈ 1.0. *Detects:* disproportionate encoding / misrepresentation. *Scoring:* continuous; flag |log(lie factor)| above a threshold. *Tufte concept (HIGH); automation is my reasoned extension (MEDIUM).*

**Dual-axis / inverted-axis / inconsistent-tick detection.** *Definition:* rule checks on axis metadata for two y-axes with different scales, axes running counter to convention, or non-uniform tick spacing. *Scoring:* binary flags per rule. *Grounded directly in Misviz linter (HIGH).*

**3D-effect detection.** *Definition:* detect perspective/extrusion cues (shadowed polygons, isometric bar faces) via CV. *Detects:* size distortion from gratuitous 3D. *Scoring:* binary classifier confidence. *3D as a misleader is established (HIGH); the CV detector is a reasoned extension (MEDIUM).*

**Aspect-ratio distortion.** *Definition:* compare the rendered aspect ratio against a "banking to 45°" optimum or a sane default band; flag extreme stretching that exaggerates or flattens trends. *Scoring:* deviation from target ratio. *Aspect ratio as a deception vector is documented — it fooled 9 of 10 VLMs in robustness testing (HIGH); banking-to-45° automation is a reasoned extension (MEDIUM).*

### Dimension 2 — Data-Ink Economy & Chartjunk (Is it cluttered or over-ornamented?)

**Data-ink ratio estimation.** *Definition:* segment the rasterized figure into data-encoding pixels (bars, lines, points) vs. non-data pixels (gridlines, borders, backgrounds, decoration), then compute data-ink / total non-background ink. *Detects:* clutter and low information density. *Scoring:* continuous 0–1; lower = more chartjunk. *Note:* practitioners rightly warn that minimalism taken to the extreme is itself absurd — this should be a flag for extremes, not a maximand. *Tufte concept is pixel-based and explicitly computable (HIGH); instance-segmentation tools that compute per-element pixel ratios already exist (HIGH); a validated end-to-end automatic estimator remains my reasoned extension (MEDIUM).*

**Gridline density.** *Definition:* count gridline pixels (or detected gridline objects) per unit plot area. *Detects:* heavy grids / moiré vibration. *Scoring:* gridline-ink fraction. *Reasoned extension from chartjunk principle (MEDIUM).*

**Decoration / non-data-element count.** *Definition:* detect drop shadows, gradient fills, background images, clip-art, and redundant 3D. *Scoring:* count + fractional area. *Chartjunk taxonomy (HIGH); detector is reasoned extension (MEDIUM).*

**Gradient/ornamentation detection.** *Definition:* detect gratuitous gradients in fills via color-variance analysis within encoding regions that should be flat. *Scoring:* fraction of encoding area with non-flat fill. *Reasoned extension (LOW–MEDIUM).*

### Dimension 3 — Perceptual & Encoding Appropriateness (Is the right chart used the right way?)

**Chart-type appropriateness.** *Definition:* given inferred data types (nominal, ordinal, quantitative, temporal) and the encoding used, score against the Cleveland–McGill perceptual ranking and Draco-style constraints (e.g., penalize pie/donut for more than ~5 categories or for precise comparison; penalize angle/area encodings where position/length would serve). *Scoring:* weighted sum of violated soft constraints (Draco "cost"). *Cleveland–McGill ranking and Draco constraints are established and explicitly computable (HIGH).*

**Color-palette entropy & perceptual uniformity.** *Definition:* for categorical encodings, measure palette distinctness (pairwise ΔE in CIELAB) and count; for continuous encodings, test the colormap for monotonic luminance / perceptual uniformity and flag rainbow/jet. *Detects:* both indistinguishable palettes and non-uniform colormaps that fabricate false detail (jet's perceptual non-uniformity creates "bands" implying structure absent from the data). *Scoring:* ΔE-based separability score; binary "rainbow-colormap" flag. *Rainbow/jet deficiency and viridis-style uniformity are well established (HIGH); the entropy formulation is a reasoned extension (MEDIUM).*

**Color-vision-deficiency safety.** *Definition:* simulate CVD transforms and re-test categorical separability. *Scoring:* min pairwise ΔE under simulation. *Established principle (HIGH); automation is standard practice (MEDIUM).*

### Dimension 4 — Legibility & Text Integrity (the strongest AI-slop tell)

This dimension is where AI generation most visibly fails and where OCR-based checks are most decisive — and where VisEval's 0.843 SRCC against experts shows automation can be trusted.

**OCR legibility / text-on-canvas check.** *Definition:* run OCR over all text regions; flag low-confidence regions, text clipped at canvas edges, and overlapping labels. *Detects:* the "text extending beyond the canvas" and overlap failures VisEval's readability dimension targets and RealChart2Code's Layout/Structural Failures category catalogs. *Scoring:* fraction of text regions below an OCR-confidence threshold; overlap area. *Grounded in VisEval readability + RealChart2Code layout failures (HIGH).*

**Garbled / hallucinated text detection.** *Definition:* pass OCR'd strings through a dictionary/language-model plausibility check; flag non-words, scrambled glyphs, repeated/duplicated letters, and nonsensical axis labels. *Detects:* the classic diffusion-model text-corruption signature and hallucinated axis labels. *Scoring:* fraction of text tokens failing a lexical/semantic plausibility test. *Garbled-text artifact is well documented for diffusion models (HIGH); the OCR→LM-plausibility pipeline is my reasoned extension (MEDIUM).*

**Label–data semantic coherence.** *Definition:* check that axis titles, units, legend entries, and tick labels are mutually consistent and consistent with the plotted data (e.g., units match magnitude; legend categories match the number of series). *Detects:* hallucinated/incoherent labels and data-mapping errors. *Scoring:* rule + LLM-judge hybrid. *Grounded in RealChart2Code "Data Mapping Errors" and "Instruction Neglect" (HIGH for the failure mode; the coherence checker is a reasoned extension, MEDIUM).*

**Typographic consistency.** *Definition:* detect the number of distinct fonts/sizes/weights used for equivalent roles; flag inconsistency. *Scoring:* count of distinct typographic styles per role. *Reasoned extension (MEDIUM); supported by RealChart2Code's "Typographic Quality" qualitative metric.*

### Dimension 5 — Generative-Artifact & Credibility Signals (Was this AI-generated, and is it fabricated?)

**Default-library-styling detection.** *Definition:* fingerprint figures against known default styles — the classic Matplotlib palette (the tab10 "C0 blue / orange" sequence), default Excel chart styling, default seaborn themes — via color-histogram and layout signatures. *Detects:* "generic/templated styling" and low-effort defaults (a slop signal under Kommers' "canonical patterns"). *Scoring:* similarity to a library of default-style templates. *The default-Matplotlib look is a documented, recognizable phenomenon (HIGH); the fingerprint detector is my reasoned extension (MEDIUM).*

**Geometry/distortion forensics.** *Definition:* for pixel-generated figures, run CV checks for impossible geometry, misaligned connectors/arrows, broken axis lines, and inconsistent perspective. *Detects:* diffusion-style structural artifacts. *Scoring:* artifact-detector confidence. *Artifact types documented (HIGH); SciFigDetect shows detectors are currently fragile in zero-shot transfer (HIGH) — so treat scores as weak evidence (MEDIUM).*

**Data-fabrication / round-trip consistency.** *Definition:* extract data values from the chart image (via a chart-understanding model) and, where source data exists, cross-validate; where it doesn't, flag implausibly smooth, too-regular, or internally inconsistent series (sums that don't add up, pie slices not summing to 100%). *Detects:* fabricated data points and the "data assignment" errors of code LLMs. *Scoring:* residual between image-extracted and source data; internal-consistency checks. *Internal-consistency checks are deterministic (HIGH); image→data extraction reliability is model-limited (MEDIUM).*

**Execution / code-level validity (when source code is available).** *Definition:* if the figure ships with generating code, execute it in a sandbox (VisEval "validity"; LIDA VER) and lint for nonexistent API calls. *Detects:* the dominant open-weight failure mode (invalid functions, hallucinated libraries). *Scoring:* pass/fail + lint-error count. *Grounded in VisEval, LIDA, RealChart2Code (HIGH).*

### Scoring architecture and validation

The five dimensions should feed a **tiered composite**, not a single number. Tier 1 (deterministic rules: truncation, dual/inverted axis, OCR-on-canvas, default-palette fingerprint) is cheap, explainable, and high-precision — run it first and surface hard flags. Tier 2 (CV/segmentation: data-ink ratio, gridline density, color entropy, geometry forensics) produces continuous scores needing calibration. Tier 3 (a calibrated multimodal LLM-as-judge panel) handles chart-type appropriateness and semantic coherence — but the 2026 evidence (VLMs fooled by axis inversion/truncation; LLM-judge leniency and position/order biases) means the judge must be (a) instrumented with explicit checklists per misleader, (b) run with chain-of-thought and written justifications, and (c) calibrated against human expert ratings.

RealChart2Code's multi-agent voting panel is a directly reusable template: it achieved high agreement (Fleiss' κ ≈ 0.82 inter-agent; Cohen's κ ≈ 0.83 agent–human) using a 3-point scale (0/1/2) across eight named criteria — chart type, spatial layout, text elements, axis configuration, color scheme, style, component completeness, and data-pattern consistency. Borrow that rubric verbatim as a starting schema for Tier 3.

## Recommendations

**Stage 1 (build now, highest ROI).** Implement the Tier-1 deterministic rule layer first: axis-truncation, dual/inverted-axis, inconsistent-tick, OCR legibility + on-canvas, garbled-text lexical check, and default-library-style fingerprinting. These have the strongest evidentiary support (Misviz linter; VisEval readability validated at 0.843 SRCC), are explainable to scientists, and catch both the most common deceptions and the loudest AI tells. Borrow the Misviz axis-metadata extraction approach and the RealChart2Code eight-criterion rubric as your starting schema.

**Stage 2 (add after Stage 1 validates).** Layer in the CV/segmentation metrics — data-ink ratio, gridline density, color entropy/perceptual-uniformity, 3D/geometry forensics. Use an instance-segmentation backbone that computes per-element pixel ratios. Calibrate thresholds against a labeled in-house set of "good" and "slop" scientific figures before trusting any continuous score. Treat data-ink ratio as a flag for *extremes*, not a target to maximize.

**Stage 3 (the judgment layer).** Add a calibrated multimodal LLM-as-judge panel for chart-type appropriateness (encoded via Draco / Cleveland–McGill constraints) and label–data semantic coherence. Instrument it with explicit per-misleader checklists, require written justifications, control for position/order bias, and measure agreement against your own expert ratings (target Cohen's κ > 0.7, benchmarked against the ≈0.83 RealChart2Code achieved, before relying on it).

**Benchmarks/thresholds that should change the plan.** If forensic AI-artifact detectors keep failing zero-shot transfer (as SciFigDetect shows), do *not* rely on "AI-generated" classification as a credibility gate — pivot to artifact-and-integrity scoring that is agnostic to provenance. If your LLM-judge agreement with experts stays below ~0.7 κ, fall back to the deterministic + CV tiers and use the judge only for triage. If image→data extraction accuracy is too low on your figure types (likely for dense multipanel scientific figures), drop round-trip fabrication checks in favor of internal-consistency checks (sums, proportions) only.

## Caveats

The provenance question and the quality question are distinct and should not be conflated: a figure can be AI-generated and excellent, or human-made and slop. The Kommers et al. position is explicitly that slop is not reducible to "AI-generated," and Shaib et al. show slop judgments are inherently subjective even among experts — so any composite "slop score" should be presented as a vector of interpretable sub-scores with flags, not a single verdict. PaperBanana's divergence (45.8% content fidelity but a 72.7% human-preference win rate) is a concrete warning that perceived polish and substantive correctness move independently; score them on separate axes.

Several of the most attractive metrics (data-ink ratio, lie factor, garbled-text detection) rest on a *recovery* step — segmentation, OCR, or chart-data extraction — whose reliability is itself model-limited and degrades on dense scientific multipanel figures; errors compound. The misleading-chart linters work on extracted axis metadata, which is straightforward for synthetic charts but harder to recover from arbitrary published bitmaps. The 2026 AI-figure-detection benchmarks (SciFigDetect) explicitly warn that detectors overfit to specific generators and break under recompression/cropping. Finally, much of the "AI slop" trade press (tool-vendor blogs, listicles) is marketing-adjacent and speculative; I have leaned on the peer-reviewed/arXiv sources for all load-bearing claims and flagged vendor commentary as such. Where I extended beyond the literature into proposed metrics, I labeled those as reasoned extensions (MEDIUM/LOW) rather than established findings, per your request to distinguish direct claims from synthesis.
