### How to catch AI reasoning errors and logical fallacies

So far in the course, we've focused on making sure the facts are correct, but we need to go beyond that. You can confirm every citation, you can check every number, and the output can still be wrong because there's a flaw in the reasoning. And this is a mistake AI tools make with alarming regularity. I've had a go at organizing some typical logic failures into categories. Here's what I think you you should watch out for. First, false correlation presented as causation. So the AI tells you, countries with higher coffee consumption have higher GDP. Therefore, coffee consumption drives economic growth. Both facts might be true, but the conclusion is nonsense. The correlation exists because wealthier countries can afford more coffee, not because coffee makes countries rich. AI can be prone to this because it identifies statistical patterns without understanding why they exist. Next, we have cherry picking evidence that ignores contrary data. The AI presents five studies supporting a conclusion and none against it. That doesn't mean there aren't any against it. It means that the AI has pattern matched towards a coherent narrative and excluded the complications. This is especially dangerous because it looks thorough. Five supporting sources feels like strong evidence until you discover there's 15 that disagree. Over-generalization from a narrow sample. A study of 200 software engineers in Silicon Valley found that flexible working improved productivity. Therefore, all companies should adopt flexible working. The study might be real, the finding might be accurate, but extending it to all companies, all industries, all cultures, and all roles is a logical leap that the evidence doesn't support. Conclusions that don't follow from the premises. This is where you need to be especially vigilant. The AI presents a series of facts that are all true and then draws a conclusion that those facts don't actually support. The conclusion might even be true for other reasons, but the argument as presented doesn't hold together. It just sounds like it does. You can't catch any of these unless you keep your brain switched on. And as I've discussed, AI output can cause people's thinking cogs to disengage. So here are three techniques to help you on your way. First, ask the AI to argue the opposite conclusion from the same evidence. If you've got an analysis saying that your company should enter a new market, prompt the AI using the same data, make the strongest possible case that we should not enter this market. If it can construct an equally convincing argument in the opposite direction, original analysis might not be as solid as it looks. Second, ask explicitly what evidence would need to exist for the conclusion to be false. This forces the AI to identify the assumptions underpinning its analysis. If it says this conclusion would be false if customer behavior in this market differs significantly from the general trend, well that's worth investigating before you commit budget to it. Third, request the strongest counter-argument before accepting any analysis. Don't just ask for the answer, ask for the best case against the answer. If the counter-argument is weak, you can proceed with more confidence. If it's strong, you've just saved yourself from a bad decision. And here's a crucial caveat. Research has shown that faulty AI reasoning doesn't just mislead you in the immediate task, it creates a false sense of understanding that persists. People who read incorrect explanations from AI don't just get the wrong answer, they develop a flawed mental model that affects their future decisions, even when the AI isn't involved. The bad reasoning gets internalized. So getting the reasoning right isn't just about one report or one decision, it's about protecting the quality of your thinking over time. ### Prompting techniques that reduce AI hallucinations

So far everything we've covered has been about catching errors after they happen, which is important, but you know, what's even better? Reducing the number of errors in the first place. You can't eliminate AI errors entirely. That's not how the technology works, but you can significantly reduce them by changing the way you prompt. Ask AI to flag its own uncertainty. Most people prompt AI with something like, tell me about X. And the AI obliges, providing a confident answer regardless of how much it actually knows. But if you add, flag any areas where you're uncertain or where the evidence is limited, the output changes. You start getting phrases like I'm less confident about this claim or the evidence here is mixed It's not perfect, but it surfaces doubts that would otherwise be buried request sources before synthesis Instead of asking what are the trends in X try find specific named studies and reports about trends in X Providing the exact titles and publication details then synthesize the findings When you force the AI to name its sources first, you make it much harder for it to confabulate. It either has a real source, or it doesn't. And if it doesn't, it's more likely to tell you. This isn't foolproof, and it may make up some citations, but it focuses the AI on research before conclusions. I wish more humans did that. Ask it to argue against its own answer. After getting an initial response, follow up with now make the strongest case against everything you just told me. This is one of the most powerful verification prompts you can use. It forces the AI to identify weaknesses in its own reasoning, surface counter evidence, and highlight assumptions. If the counter argument is weak, your confidence in the original answer goes up. If it's strong, you've just found something worth investigating. Specify that hedged answers are acceptable and even preferred. Medium-stakes actions. Sending emails, updating shared documents, posting content. The AI should propose and you should approve. High-stakes actions. Financial transactions, legal filings, anything irreversible. You need to be in the driving seat with AI as your assistant. So always ask three questions before giving an AI the ability to act on your behalf. What's the worst thing it could do with this access? Would I know if it did something wrong? And can I undo it? Some of you could undo the damage. e-mail deletion is usually recoverable. But imagine if the AI had been sending emails or making financial transactions, or publishing content, or deploying code. That's like trying to put the toothpaste back in the tube. Finally, and this builds on everything we've discussed about verification, when AI takes actions, you need audit trails. You need to be able to see what it did, when it did it, and the reasoning that led to the outcome. If you can't reconstruct the chain of actions after the fact, you've got no way of reliably avoiding it in the future. The tools are getting more capable every month. That's genuinely exciting. But capability without oversight is how you end up sprinting back home to unplug something before it deletes your entire inbox. The verification skills you've built in this course should become a mindset. You need to get into the habit of questioning the output, checking the work, and when the AI is doing things rather than saying things, make sure that there's a human in the loop before anything irreversible happens. Because whatever the AI does, your name is still on it and you hold the ultimate responsibility. Even if you've inadvertently sold all of the office furniture on eBay. Don't do that.



## Checking the Actions of Agents
### When AI errors go beyond words to actions

We've covered a lot of ground. Now we need to turn all of that into something that you'll actually do on a Tuesday afternoon when you're tired and the deadline is in 40 minutes. Because the problem with most verification advice is that it sounds great in theory, but falls apart under real-world pressure. A 40-point checklist is useless if you never use it. What you need is a prioritized sequence that scales with the stakes and fits into the time that you actually have. So here's the workflow, three steps. Step one, risk triage. Before you verify anything, ask yourself one question. What happens if this is wrong? If the answer is not much, maybe it's an internal brainstorming document or a first draft that'll go through several rounds of review, then a quick scan for obvious red flags is probably sufficient. Use the instincts I gave you earlier. Does anything smell off? If not, move on. If the answer is it would be embarrassing, a client-facing e-mail, a presentation to senior leadership, a published article, then you need to verify the claims. Check the numbers, confirm the sources exist, read the reasoning with fresh eyes. And if the answer is, it could cause real damage, a legal document, a financial analysis, a medical recommendation, a public statement, then you verify everything. Every fact, every source, every logical step, no shortcuts. The point is proportionate effort. Not everything needs the same level of scrutiny. But you need to make that judgment call consciously, not let time pressure make it for you. Step two, verify by type. Once you've decided something needs checking, work through it in this order. Numbers and statistics first, because these are the easiest to verify and the most dangerous when wrong. Search for the specific claim, find the original source, check whether the number matches or if it was fabricated. This takes seconds per claim and catches the most common fabrication type. Citations and sources next. Does the cited study, report, or article actually exist? Can you find it? Does it say what the AI claims it says? A quick search will usually tell you. If you can't find it within a couple of minutes, treat it as suspect. Then, reasoning and logic. This is the hardest part, and the one most people skip. Read the argument as if you're looking for holes. Does the conclusion follow from the evidence? Are there assumptions that haven't been stated? Would the evidence support a different conclusion equally well? Use the techniques I showed you to spot logic errors. Step three, the second opinion. Once you've done your own review, consider running the output through a different AI model and asking it to critique the first one's work. Something like, here's an analysis. Identify any factual errors, unsupported claims, logical fallacies, or missing perspectives. A second AI model can catch some errors that the first one missed, but it can also miss the same ones or introduce new ones of its own. So treat it as an additional check, not as a replacement for your own judgment. And that's it. triage, verify by type, get a second opinion. The whole thing can take five minutes for low-stakes content and 30 minutes for something critical. It scales with the situation, it respects your time, and it protects your reputation. And that's what this entire course has been about. Not slowing you down, not making you distrust your tools, but giving you the skills to use them confidently and the knowledge that you're not playing buckaroo with your career. Oh! Jeez.
