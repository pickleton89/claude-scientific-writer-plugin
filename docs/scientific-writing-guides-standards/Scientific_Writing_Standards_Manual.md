# Scientific Writing Standards Manual
## A Comprehensive Framework for the Working Scientist

---

## I. Foundational Principles: The Reader-Centric Mandate

Scientific writing is not a historical record of laboratory activity. It is an act of persuasion — an argument designed to transfer complex information into the brain of a skeptical, time-constrained reader with minimal cognitive friction (Zhang, 2014). When a reader struggles to parse a sentence, they are not merely losing time; they are losing trust. Confusion breeds skepticism, and if the prose is messy, the reader may unconsciously assume the science is equally imprecise.

The defining characteristic of excellent scientific prose is what Steven Pinker (2014) calls "classic style": the writer acts as a guide, directing the reader's gaze toward a shared reality. This stands in sharp contrast to the defensive, self-conscious register known as "academese," where authors obscure meaning behind abstractions to signal intellectual seriousness to their peers. Classic style assumes the reader is intelligent but lacks your specific context. Every word must earn its place by serving that reader's understanding — not the writer's ego.

### The Three Pillars

Three principles anchor all scientific prose. **Clarity** ensures that sentences are immediately understandable without ambiguity. **Conciseness** respects the reader's time by eliminating every word that does not add meaning. **Accuracy** provides a precise, correct representation of data and methodology. These are not independent goals; they reinforce one another. A concise sentence is almost always clearer than a verbose one, and a clear sentence is far more likely to be accurate because it forces the writer to confront exactly what the data support.

### The Rule of Parsimony

Every detail included must be essential to supporting the central conclusion. If a methodological nuance or a secondary result does not contribute to the narrative, it should be relegated to supplementary materials or removed entirely (Zhang, 2014). Traditional academic communication often resembles a sledgehammer — global, slow, and non-specific. Scientific writing must be a precision instrument. This is not about suppressing complexity; it is about managing it. The question for every sentence is: does this serve the reader's journey from question to answer?

### The Curse of Knowledge

Scientific writers routinely fall victim to what Pinker calls the "curse of knowledge" — the inability to imagine what it is like not to know something you have already mastered. This cognitive blind spot drives the overuse of undefined jargon, abstractions without referents, and throat-clearing phrases that delay the point. The antidote is deliberate perspective-taking: before finalizing any section, ask whether a colleague in an adjacent subfield could follow the argument without reaching for a textbook. Because human brains evolved to process narratives, framing your research as a story — with your study system as the main character, the literature as the setting, the knowledge gap as the tension, and your findings as the resolution — is the most effective way to communicate data (Bauman, 2023).

> **Weak:** It is important to note that the data gathered from our experiments showed a significant increase in cell death.
>
> **Revised:** Cell death increased significantly.

## II. Argument Architecture: The Narrative Arc of IMRAD

The IMRAD structure (Introduction, Methods, Results, and Discussion) is not merely a set of buckets for data. It mirrors the scientific method itself and constitutes a logical progression where each section serves a distinct argumentative function optimized for the reader's journey (Zhang, 2014).

### The Introduction: Funnel and Gap

A successful Introduction operates as a funnel, opening with the broad research area and narrowing systematically to the specific gap in current knowledge. Schimel (2012) formalizes this as the **OCAR structure**: the **Opening** establishes the characters (your study system), the **Challenge** identifies the conflict (the gap in knowledge), the **Action** describes what you did, and the **Resolution** previews the outcome. In practice, the Introduction should follow a four-paragraph architectural template:

1. **The Big Picture.** Establish the broad research area and why it matters. This is the opening that earns the reader's attention.
2. **Prior Research (1–2 paragraphs).** Review the relevant literature, showing what is known while narrowing toward a specific problem. This is not an exhaustive literature review; it provides only the context necessary to establish tension.
3. **The Gap.** Explicitly identify what remains unknown and why this lack of knowledge is problematic. The gap sentence is the fulcrum of the entire paper.
4. **This Study.** State the hypothesis and how this work addresses the identified gap. The reader should know exactly what to expect before reaching the Methods.

At a higher level, the narrative arc of the entire paper can be organized around Bauman's (2023) **ABT framework**: the **And** (context — what we know), the **But** (the problem or gap), and the **Therefore** (the solution or finding). Narrative should guide the structure; precision guides the sentence-level word choice.

### Methods: The Reproducibility Contract

The Methods section serves a fundamentally different argumentative purpose: proving that the work can be replicated. An expert peer must be able to repeat the experiment exactly from what is written. This requires a tactical balance: provide exhaustive detail (reagents, manufacturers, catalog numbers, software versions) for novel or critical steps, but cite established protocols for routine procedures. Use specific subheadings (Study Design, Participants, Statistical Analysis) to organize complexity. Passive voice is often appropriate here because the action matters more than the actor — but clarity remains paramount.

### Results: Show, Don't Interpret

The Results section delivers the narrative thread of the data objectively. The mandate is to show the reader what happened without drifting into interpretation; save that for the Discussion. Present findings in the order of the objectives stated in the Introduction, not in the order they were collected. Each finding should include the observed result, the direction and magnitude of the effect, and the statistical significance:

> Mean systolic blood pressure decreased significantly in the intervention group compared to controls (*t*(48) = 3.21, *p* = 0.002, Cohen's *d* = 0.87, 95% CI: 0.34–1.40).

### Discussion: The "So What?" and "What Next?" Layers

The Discussion resolves the narrative tension by answering two questions. The **"So What?" layer** interprets the findings in context — what do they mean for the field? The **"What Next?" layer** proposes mechanistic explanations, acknowledges limitations honestly, and charts future directions. The single most common failure pattern in scientific writing is using the Discussion merely to restate the results. Instead, it must contextualize the findings within the broader literature and articulate the theoretical or clinical implications.

Apply the **C-C-C framework** to each Discussion paragraph: the **Context** sentence summarizes the relevant finding, the **Content** sentences compare it to prior work and propose mechanisms, and the **Conclusion** sentence states the implications.

### The Writing Order

Do not write the paper from top to bottom. Establish your figures and tables first, then draft the Results and Methods. Save the Introduction, Discussion, and Abstract for last, when the story's trajectory is fully clear (Sainani, SciWrite 5.1). This sequence prevents the common trap of writing an Introduction that frames a story your data cannot actually tell.

## III. Sentence and Paragraph Craft: The Mechanics of Clarity

Sentence-level clarity is the difference between a paper that is skimmed and one that is read. When writing mechanics are flawed, the reader begins to question the rigor of the underlying science.

### Paragraph Architecture

Each paragraph must contain exactly one main idea (Sainani, SciWrite 3.4). Use the **Context-Content-Conclusion** framework to ensure every paragraph orients the reader, delivers new evidence, and provides a clear takeaway.

Critically, you should invert the typical scientific instinct to build up through endless details to a buried conclusion. Give the punchline early, then follow with the supporting evidence. The reader should never reach the end of a paragraph wondering "what was the point of that?"

Paragraphs must connect to one another through a visible "red thread." Use signposting words — *however, moreover, consequently, in contrast* — to indicate the logical relationship between ideas. Barnett et al. (2021) found that clarity-promoting features like signposting and punctuation variety significantly build reader confidence.

### Voice and Tense

Modern standards encourage **active voice** as the default because the English language relies on Subject-Verb-Object progression, and academics frequently invert this into the passive, omitting the actor entirely (Sainani, SciWrite 2.1). Active voice provides vigor, directness, and accountability. However, **passive voice** remains the appropriate choice in the Methods section, where the action matters more than the actor. The resolution is contextual: default to active voice in the Introduction, Results, and Discussion; employ passive voice in Methods when describing procedures (Rodrigues, 2020).

Tense usage must be section-specific:

| Section | Recommended Tense |
|---|---|
| Introduction | Present for established facts; past for prior studies |
| Methods | Past for actions performed; present for general procedures |
| Results | Past for specific findings |
| Discussion | Past for findings; present for interpretations and accepted facts |
| Conclusion | Present for the take-home message |

### The War on Nominalization

Helen Sword (2012) identifies "zombie nouns" — nominalizations that embalm active verbs into heavy, stagnant nouns. Nominalizations require pairing a weak generic verb with a ponderous noun, draining prose of agency and energy.

> **Weak:** We conducted an analysis of the proteins to achieve a reduction in errors.
>
> **Revised:** We analyzed the proteins to reduce errors.

> **Weak:** A determination of the protein concentration was performed.
>
> **Revised:** We determined the protein concentration.

Do not write that a study "provides a confirmation of" a hypothesis; write that it "confirms" it. Revise "conduct an assessment of" to "assess." Change "make an adjustment to" to "adjust."

### The Paramedic Method

When a sentence feels bloated but you cannot immediately see why, apply the **Paramedic Method**:

1. Circle the prepositions (*of, in, about, for, onto, into*).
2. Circle the "to be" forms (*is, am, are, was, were*).
3. Ask: "Where is the action?"
4. Change the action into a simple verb.

This systematic approach converts passive, prepositionally dense sentences into direct prose.

### Managing Density and Clutter

Cut throat-clearing introductory phrases that dilute your claims: *"It is important to note that," "As it is well known," "It has long been recognized that."* These add nothing. The sentence that follows them is invariably the one that should have opened the paragraph.

> **Weak:** The activation of channels is induced by the depletion of stores.
>
> **Revised:** Depleting stores activates channels.

## IV. Precision and Word Choice: The Ethics of Accuracy

In science, words are tools for precision. Choosing the wrong verb can misrepresent the strength of your evidence, and inconsistent terminology can derail the reader entirely.

### The Banana Rule

Once you establish a term for a variable, group, or concept, you must use that exact term throughout the manuscript. Do not cycle through synonyms to avoid repetition. If the Methods section introduces an "obese group," the Results section must not suddenly refer to a "heavier group" — this forces the reader to wonder whether a new variable has been introduced. In scientific writing, elegant variation is the enemy of clarity. If you called it a banana in the Introduction, it is still a banana in the Discussion.

### The Spectrum of Hedging

Scientific integrity requires saying exactly what the data support — no more, no less. Authors must navigate between two failure modes: **death-by-qualification** (hedging so aggressively that the claim evaporates) and **unwarranted causal claims** (overstating correlational evidence).

Use verbs that match the strength of your evidence:

- **Definitive evidence** (controlled experiments with clear causation): *demonstrates, establishes, shows*
- **Strong but indirect evidence** (consistent results, plausible mechanisms): *indicates, reveals, confirms*
- **Suggestive evidence** (correlations, preliminary data): *suggests, implies, is consistent with*
- **Speculative interpretation**: *may, might, could potentially*

Reserve *demonstrates* and *proves* for definitive, high-confidence data. Use *suggests* and *indicates* for data that point toward a conclusion but allow for alternative explanations. Excessive hedging makes you sound uncertain; insufficient hedging makes you sound unscientific.

> **Over-hedged:** It could potentially possibly be argued that the policy might have some effect.
>
> **Revised:** The policy may affect outcomes.

### Jargon and Acronym Discipline

Distinguish between **precise jargon** that serves as efficient shorthand for experts and **pretentious jargon** used merely to sound sophisticated (Pinker, 2014). The readability crisis in scientific literature is driven primarily by general scientific jargon — words used by scientists that substitute for adequate common terms (Plavén-Sigray et al., 2017). Define essential technical terms immediately upon first use. For acronyms, follow the **Rule of 3–4**: only abbreviate terms used four or more times, and avoid abbreviations in the title and abstract whenever possible.

### Lexical Quick-Reference

| Pair | Correct Usage |
|---|---|
| Affect vs. Effect | *Affect* (verb): to influence. *Effect* (noun): a result. |
| Fewer vs. Less | *Fewer*: countable items (cells, patients). *Less*: continuous quantities (mass, time). |
| That vs. Which | *That*: restrictive clause (essential to meaning). *Which*: nonrestrictive (extra information, set off by commas). |
| Data are | "Data" is plural. "Data are consistent" — not "data is." |

## V. Figures, Tables, and Text Integration: Visual Persuasion

Figures and tables are not illustrations; they are the primary evidence of your argument and often the first elements a peer reviewer will examine (Sainani, SciWrite 5.1). They must be designed as freestanding arguments.

### The Independence Test

Every figure must pass the **Independence Test**: can someone understand the key finding just by looking at the figure and reading the caption, without hunting through the main text? Each figure should communicate a clear, one-sentence message. Captions must define all abbreviations, specify sample sizes (*n*), identify statistical tests used, and include units of measurement. If it fails the Independence Test, rewrite the caption before touching the main text.

### Integration with Prose

The main text must not serve as an audio tour reading every data point in a table line by line. That is redundant and dulls the reader's attention. Instead, use the text to guide interpretation, highlight significant trends, and provide complementary comparisons:

> **Redundant:** The control group scored 30 and the treatment group scored 60.
>
> **Interpretive:** The treatment group doubled the performance of the control group (Figure 2).

Use specific callouts: "As shown in Figure 1, the treatment group showed a twofold increase..." rather than the empty "Figure 1 shows the results."

### Data-Ink Ratio

Maximize the ratio of data to non-data ink. Remove three-dimensional effects, background gradients, unnecessary gridlines, and decorative elements that distract from the scientific message. Every visual element in a figure should encode data or provide essential context — nothing more.

## VI. Abstracts, Titles, and First Impressions: The Craft of Compression

The title and abstract form the critical first impression of your work and dictate its discoverability. They determine whether a scientist clicks "Download" or "Next."

### The Flowing Paragraph Abstract

Unless a target journal explicitly demands a structured abstract with bolded subheadings (as BMJ and JAMA do), write the abstract as a single, flowing, cohesive paragraph. Labeled sections fragment the narrative and prevent the kind of argumentative momentum that makes an abstract compelling. Structure it internally with four elements:

1. **Context:** The gap in knowledge (1–2 sentences).
2. **Methods:** The core approach (1–2 sentences).
3. **Findings:** Specific quantitative results — this is the heart of the abstract.
4. **Significance:** The take-home message.

**Write the abstract last.** Only after the full manuscript exists can you accurately compress the story. Never cite references within the abstract, and ensure that every number matches the data reported in the main text and tables exactly — a mismatch here severely undermines author credibility (Sainani, SciWrite 4.6). The abstract must function as a completely standalone document.

### Strategic Titles

Titles should be concise but descriptive — aim for 10–15 words. A strong title contains the key finding or the relationship studied, includes the specific keywords researchers will type into search engines, and avoids abbreviations. Eliminate empty openings: *"A Study of...," "Observations on...," "Towards an Understanding of..."* add no information.

### The Discoverability Paradox

Standard writing advice dictates ruthless brevity in all things. However, Weinberger et al. (2015) reveal an empirical paradox: shorter, overly simplified abstracts actually receive fewer citations. Abstracts rich in specific adjectives, adverbs, and robust technical detail achieve higher citation impact — likely because terminology-dense prose performs better in academic search engine algorithms. The resolution: write the **main body** with uncompromising conciseness for human readability, while allowing the **title and abstract** to remain dense with specific, descriptive keywords to optimize for machine discoverability.

## VII. Grant Writing: The Persuasive Register

Grant writing is scientific writing operating in a fundamentally different register. A manuscript reports completed work; a grant proposes work that does not yet exist. The audience — a review panel scanning dozens of proposals under time pressure — demands an even more aggressive approach to clarity and persuasion than journal peer review.

### The Specific Aims Page

The Specific Aims page is the single most important page of any NIH-style grant. It must accomplish in one page what an Introduction does in several: establish the big picture, identify the gap, present the central hypothesis, and lay out the specific aims that will test it. The aims should be logically connected but not serially dependent — if Aim 1 fails, Aim 2 must still be meaningful.

Structure the Aims page as a funnel: two to three paragraphs narrowing from field significance to the specific gap, followed by a clear statement of the central hypothesis, then numbered aims with one-sentence descriptions. Close with a brief impact statement explaining how successful completion changes the field.

### Significance vs. Innovation vs. Approach

NIH review criteria separate Significance (does the problem matter?), Innovation (does this offer something new?), and Approach (is the plan rigorous?). Writers commonly underweight Significance, treating it as a box-checking exercise rather than the framing device it should be. The Significance section is where you make the reviewer *care*. It must answer "So What?" before the reviewer has a chance to ask it.

### Writing for the Reviewer's Experience

Grant reviewers read fast and assign scores based on overall impression as much as on specific details. Front-load every section with its most important sentence. Use bold text and white space strategically to create visual entry points. Anticipate criticisms and address them preemptively — a concern raised by the reviewer that the writer clearly already considered and addressed builds confidence; the same concern left unaddressed destroys it.

Feasibility is the silent criterion underlying everything. Preliminary data is not merely supportive evidence — it is proof of concept that the team can execute the proposed work. Frame preliminary data as demonstrating feasibility, not merely as interesting results.

## VIII. Editing and Revision: The Scientific Diagnostic

Great writing is not generated; it is rewritten. Professional writers spend the vast majority of their time in the revision phase, and you should expect to do the same (Sainani, SciWrite 4.2). Treat drafting and editing as strictly separate cognitive tasks to avoid creative paralysis. In the first draft, silence the inner critic and focus entirely on getting ideas down in a logical order. Elegance comes later.

### The Revision Hierarchy

Revision must proceed top-down. It is tempting to start polishing sentences, but sentence-level editing is wasted effort if the section belongs somewhere else — or nowhere at all.

1. **Structure.** Does the logical flow hold? Are the sections in the right order? Is the "red thread" visible from Introduction to Discussion?
2. **Paragraphs.** Does each paragraph contain exactly one main idea? Is the C-C-C framework intact? Are transitions smooth?
3. **Sentences.** Are they concise? Is the voice appropriate for the section? Are nominalizations eliminated?
4. **Words.** Is the terminology consistent (Banana Rule)? Are the numbers checked? Is the hedging calibrated?

### The Read-Aloud Diagnostic

Reading the manuscript aloud is one of the most effective revision tools available. The ear catches repetitive cadences, buried leads, and clumsy syntax that the eye glosses over (Sainani, SciWrite 4.5). If you stumble while reading a sentence aloud, the reader will stumble too.

### The Five Pre-Submission Tests

Before submission, perform these diagnostic checks:

1. **The Elevator Pitch Test.** Can the main finding be understood from the Title and Abstract alone? FAIL if the conclusion is buried in the middle of the abstract.
2. **The Independence Test.** Print the figures and captions alone. FAIL if abbreviations are not defined in the caption or if the main finding is not apparent.
3. **The "So What?" Test.** Read the Discussion. FAIL if it lacks an explicit statement of broader significance.
4. **The Consistency Test.** Verify that terminology, abbreviations, and variable names remain standardized across all sections.
5. **The Numerical Consistency Test.** Do the numbers in the Abstract match those in the Results, Tables, and Figure legends exactly? A single discrepancy here — a percentage that rounds differently, a sample size that shifted — severely undermines credibility with reviewers.

## IX. Principles in Tension: The Rhetorical Judgment Call

Scientific writing is governed by competing conventions. There is no universal right answer for many stylistic choices — only context-dependent resolutions.

### Active vs. Passive Voice

**The tension:** Active voice is more concise and clear; passive voice is traditionally seen as more "objective." **The resolution:** Use active voice as the default for your findings and conclusions ("We found," "This suggests"). Use passive voice in the Methods when the action is more important than the actor ("Samples were centrifuged at 12,000 × *g*"). Voice should be a deliberate rhetorical choice — active for impact, passive for procedural neutrality (Rodrigues, 2020).

### Narrative vs. Scrupulous Precision

**The tension:** Storytelling improves engagement; scrupulous hedging prioritizes every nuance at the expense of flow. **The resolution:** Narrative should guide the *structure* of the paper (the ABT framework), while precision guides the *sentence-level word choice*. Storytelling is essential for research impact, but it must be built upon a foundation of rigor (Bauman, 2023). You can tell a compelling story and still be precise — these are not mutually exclusive goals.

### Conciseness vs. Discoverability

**The tension:** Standard writing advice demands ruthless brevity; empirical evidence shows that overly simplified abstracts receive fewer citations (Weinberger et al., 2015). **The resolution:** Optimize for two different audiences. Write the main body with uncompromising conciseness for human readers. Allow the title and abstract to carry specific, descriptive, keyword-rich language that academic search algorithms reward. Brevity in the body; density in the metadata.

### First Person ("We")

**The tension:** Some authors feel "We" is informal; others argue it provides necessary agency. **The resolution:** Most modern journals prefer first person when describing the authors' actions or intellectual contributions. It avoids awkward constructions like "It was observed by the authors that..." Use first person to claim responsibility for the intellectual leaps in the paper — the experimental design decisions, the interpretive choices, the novel arguments.

### Classic Style vs. Defensive Writing

**The tension:** Classic style prizes transparency and directness; the reality of peer review often forces a degree of defensive self-presentation where every claim is preemptively qualified. **The resolution:** Write the first draft in classic style — direct, confident, clear. Then add hedging strategically during revision, only where the data genuinely warrant uncertainty. This approach preserves readability while satisfying the peer review expectation of appropriate qualification. Never hedge preemptively out of anxiety; hedge because the evidence requires it.

## X. Writing Style by Venue: Adapting Your Register

Scientific style is not monolithic. The same dataset demands different prose depending on the target venue. Calibrating your register to the audience is not pandering — it is reader-centric writing in practice.

| Aspect | Nature / Science | Medical (JAMA / NEJM) | ML Conferences (NeurIPS) | NIH Grant (R01) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Audience** | Broad scientists | Clinicians | Researchers / engineers | Review panel (mixed expertise) |
| **Tone** | Engaging, significance-driven | Conservative, formal | Direct, contribution-focused | Persuasive, feasibility-driven |
| **Key feature** | Compelling story | Clinical outcomes | Numbered contributions | Significance + rigor |
| **Methods** | Often in supplement | Comprehensive in main text | Concise / pseudocode | Exhaustive with alternatives |
| **Abstract style** | Flowing paragraph | Structured (labeled sections) | Flowing paragraph | Specific Aims page replaces abstract |
| **Word limits** | Severe (~3,000 words) | Moderate (~3,500) | Strict page limits (8–9 pp.) | 12 pages (Research Strategy) |

## Appendix: Common Failure Patterns

These are the recurring problems that weaken otherwise sound scientific writing. Each is a violation of one or more principles in this manual.

**The Buried Lead.** The most important finding appears in the third paragraph of the Discussion instead of the first sentence. Readers who skim — and all reviewers skim — will miss it entirely.

**The Method-Heavy Introduction.** The Introduction drifts into methodological detail before establishing why the reader should care. The gap must come before the approach.

**The Restating Discussion.** The Discussion merely recapitulates the Results in slightly different words, never reaching the "So What?" layer. If a Discussion paragraph could be moved to the Results section without seeming out of place, it has failed.

**The Synonym Carousel.** The writer uses three different terms for the same variable across three sections, forcing the reader to track whether "the treatment group," "the intervention cohort," and "the experimental arm" refer to the same thing. They always do. Pick one.

**The Orphan Statistic.** A *p*-value is reported without the effect size, confidence interval, or even the direction of the effect. Statistical significance without biological or clinical context is meaningless.

**The Empty Callout.** "Figure 3 shows the results" conveys nothing. "Figure 3 shows that phosphorylation peaked at 30 minutes and declined to baseline by 2 hours" tells the reader what to see and why it matters.
