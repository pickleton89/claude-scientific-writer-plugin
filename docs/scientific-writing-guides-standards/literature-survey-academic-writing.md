# Literature Survey: Writing and Writing Style for Academic Outputs (2015–2025)

*Compiled March 2026*

---

## 1. Introduction and Scope

This survey covers the peer-reviewed literature and key scholarly works from the past decade addressing how academics write—and how they might write better. The literature clusters into several distinct but overlapping themes: empirical studies of readability trends, the relationship between writing style and citation impact, narrative and storytelling approaches, the active/passive voice debate, influential style guides and frameworks, and the rapidly emerging literature on AI-assisted academic writing. What emerges is a field undergoing genuine transformation, driven by both data and technology.

---

## 2. The Readability Crisis: Scientific Writing Is Getting Harder to Read

The single most-cited empirical finding in this space comes from **Plavén-Sigray et al. (2017)**, published in *eLife*. Analyzing 709,577 abstracts from 123 scientific journals published between 1881 and 2015, the authors demonstrated a steady decline in readability using both Flesch Reading Ease (FRE) and New Dale-Chall (NDC) scores. The primary driver was not multi-authorship (though that contributes) but the proliferation of "general scientific jargon"—words used almost exclusively by scientists that substitute for perfectly adequate common terms. Strikingly, over one-fifth of abstracts were written at a level that even a college graduate would struggle to comprehend.

This finding has been replicated and extended several times:

- **Graf-Vlachy (2021)** confirmed the same trend across ~28,000 management research abstracts (1991–2019).
- **Vergoulis et al. (2019)** analyzed ~12 million multidisciplinary abstracts (1900–2018) and found the same declining trajectory.
- **Barnett & Doubleday (2020)**, in *eLife*, examined 24 million article titles and 18 million abstracts in health and medicine (1950–2019) and documented a tenfold increase in acronym density—from 0.4 per 100 words to 4 per 100 words—with 79% of acronyms used fewer than 10 times in the literature.
- **Ryba et al. (2025)**, in *Scientometrics*, extended the analysis to 20 million abstracts over 70 years, finding positive trends (increased use of active narration and verbs, decreased hedging) but also worrying increases in noun chunks and number-heavy prose.

The practical consequences of poor readability are tangible. **Weinberger et al. (2015)** and **Hillier et al. (2016)** both found correlations between writing accessibility and citation impact. Research by the *Nature Index* noted that jargon in titles and abstracts significantly reduces citation counts.

## 3. Writing Style, Reader Confidence, and Citation Impact

A pivotal experimental study by **Barnett, Doubleday, and colleagues (2021)**, published in *Frontiers in Psychology* ("Better Writing in Scientific Publications Builds Reader Confidence and Understanding"), moved beyond correlation to causation. They created multiple versions of real scientific abstracts at four levels of stylistic difficulty and tested them on randomized participants. The results were striking: mean reader confidence was 62% for accessible-style abstracts versus 49% for traditional-style ones. More readable writing also enhanced comprehension.

The study identified nine measurable writing components that affect clarity, drawn from psychology, English, and science communication literature:

- **Clarity-promoting features**: signposting, setting/context, first-person narration, conjunctions, punctuation variety
- **Clarity-reducing features**: excessive acronyms, noun chunks ("nominalization chains"), unnecessary hedging, extra/diluting words

These components built on the earlier observational work of **Ryba et al. (2019)**, which derived the framework from cross-disciplinary findings.

**Song et al. (2023)**, in the *Journal of Informetrics*, examined writing styles across information science and library science journals over three decades, finding that linguistic features (lexical density, syntactic complexity, cohesion) correlate with both journal impact factor quartile and knowledge diffusion patterns—with inverted U-shaped relationships suggesting an optimal level of linguistic sophistication.

A related computational study found that journals with lower impact factors exhibit higher levels of "imprecision indicators" such as citation bunches (strings of references without engagement) and overuse of hedging/relativating words.

## 4. Storytelling and Narrative in Scientific Communication

The argument for narrative structure in scientific writing has been building steadily. **Dahlstrom (2014)**, in *PNAS*, provided the theoretical foundation: narratives are easier to comprehend and more engaging than traditional logical-scientific communication, and the sources from which nonexperts receive most science information are already biased toward narrative formats.

Key works in this thread include:

- **Olson, R. (2015)**. *Houston, We Have a Narrative: Why Science Needs Story*. University of Chicago Press. Olson introduced the "ABT" (And, But, Therefore) framework—perhaps the most widely adopted narrative tool in scientific writing workshops. The "And" establishes context, the "But" introduces tension/problem, and the "Therefore" presents the solution.
- **Schimel, J. (2012)**. *Writing Science: How to Write Papers That Get Cited and Proposals That Get Funded*. Oxford University Press. Schimel popularized the "OCAR" structure (Opening, Challenge, Action, Resolution) and the idea that every paper tells a story with characters (your study system), conflict (the knowledge gap), and resolution (your findings).
- **Bauman (2023)**, in *Nature Reviews Cancer*, argued that storytelling is essential for maximizing research impact across diverse audiences and for clarifying the researcher's own thinking.
- **Clemens, A. (various blog posts and workshops, 2019–2025)** translated classic storytelling elements (character, setting, tension, action, climax, resolution) into the IMRaD framework, with practical guidance on maintaining a single main theme and logical chronology.
- **Bloomfield, E.F. (2024)**. *Science V. Story: Narrative Strategies for Science Communicators*. University of California Press. A more recent treatment of the tension between scientific rigor and narrative engagement.

An important counterpoint comes from the storytelling-as-research-method literature. **McCall et al. (2021)**, in the *International Journal of Public Health*, conducted a systematic narrative review of storytelling used as both research tool and intervention, finding 3,077 studies but noting that rigorous evaluation of storytelling's effectiveness remains sparse.

## 5. The Active vs. Passive Voice Debate

This remains one of the most discussed stylistic questions in scientific writing. The empirical picture has become clearer:

- **Amdur, Kirwan & Morris** found that in high-impact medical journals, the median frequency of passive-voice sentences was 20–26%, though some articles reached 46–47%.
- **Inzunza (2020)** demonstrated correlations between passive voice usage and perceptions of objectivity versus clarity.
- Multiple major journals (including *Nature*, *Science*, and the *American Journal of Botany*) now explicitly instruct authors to prefer active voice in their guidelines.

The emerging consensus, articulated well in a 2020 essay by **Rodrigues** ("Reconsidering the Use of the Passive Voice in Scientific Writing"), is pragmatic rather than dogmatic: active voice is generally preferable for conciseness and clarity, but passive voice remains appropriate when the action (not the actor) should be emphasized—particularly in Methods sections. The key insight is that voice should be a deliberate rhetorical choice, not a default habit.

## 6. Influential Books and Frameworks

The past decade has produced several landmark works that serve as both theoretical analyses and practical guides:

- **Sword, H. (2012)**. *Stylish Academic Writing*. Harvard University Press. Sword analyzed over 1,000 peer-reviewed articles across disciplines and documented the gap between what academics describe as good writing and what they actually produce. She coined the memorable term "zombie nouns" for nominalizations that drain prose of agency and life. Her empirical finding that a "healthy minority" in every field write with clarity and verve debunked the myth that journals demand turgid prose.
- **Sword, H. (2017)**. *Air & Light & Time & Space: How Successful Academics Write*. Harvard University Press. Expanded the investigation to writing habits and environments.
- **Pinker, S. (2014)**. *The Sense of Style: The Thinking Person's Guide to Writing in the 21st Century*. Viking. Pinker brought cognitive science to bear on the writing problem, identifying two key explanatory concepts: (1) the **"curse of knowledge"**—experts cannot easily reconstruct what it's like not to know something, leading them to write in abstractions and jargon that feel clear to them but are opaque to readers; and (2) **"classic style"**—a prose ideal where the writer acts as a guide showing the reader something in the world, maintaining a conversational equality. Pinker argued that much academic writing adopts "self-conscious style" instead, where the writer's overriding goal is defensive self-presentation rather than communication.
- **Silvia, P.J. (2019, 2nd ed.)**. *How to Write a Lot: A Practical Guide to Productive Academic Writing*. APA. Focused on the behavioral/productivity side of writing rather than style per se, but widely influential.
- **Williams, J. & Bizup, J. (2017, 12th ed.)**. *Style: Lessons in Clarity and Grace*. Pearson. The most systematic treatment of sentence-level clarity, with particular emphasis on diagnosing and fixing nominalization, weak verbs, and unclear agency.
- **Behzadi & Gajdács (2021)**, in *Biological Futura*, published a practical checklist for writing strong scientific papers, covering the full manuscript from title through references.

A critical perspective comes from **Bacon (2015)**, in *College Composition and Communication*, who reviewed both Sword and Pinker and argued that neither engaged sufficiently with composition studies research. Bacon proposed a taxonomy of style (individual, house, usage, plain, elaborated) paralleling Hartwell's taxonomy of grammars.

An even sharper critique appeared on the *Metaphor Hacker* blog (2021), arguing that Sword and Pinker overemphasize prose aesthetics at the expense of the primary function of academic writing: enabling peers to identify, evaluate, and debate arguments within disciplinary conventions. This "scrupulous over stylish" position has adherents, particularly in fields where precision trumps accessibility.

## 7. Reducing Wordiness and Enhancing Conciseness

A practical thread in the literature addresses the mechanics of trimming prose. **Kurihara et al. (2024)**, in *PMC*, catalogued specific strategies for reducing wordiness: eliminating redundancy, removing purposeless phrases ("it may be said that," "it was found that"), limiting excessive adjectives and adverbs, and preferring active constructions. The authors noted that non-native English-speaking researchers often use filler phrases that mimic patterns from their native language.

The "paramedic method" (Lanham, originally 1992, widely cited in recent writing pedagogy) provides a systematic sentence-level editing procedure: identify the main action, find the real subject, and rewrite to put the subject before the verb and the verb before the object. Recent treatments emphasize that conciseness is partly a matter of style—overly terse prose can feel dry—but that conscious decisions about word economy produce more readable results than unconscious verbosity.

## 8. AI and the Transformation of Academic Writing (2023–Present)

The release of ChatGPT in late 2022 triggered an explosion of literature on AI-assisted academic writing. Key threads include:

**Benefits and opportunities:**
- AI tools can serve as equalizers for non-native English speakers, helping with grammar, syntax, and adherence to disciplinary conventions (Cai et al., 2024; Kousha & Thelwall, 2025; Nugroho et al., 2024). Language enhancement is the most commonly acknowledged use when scholars cite ChatGPT.
- AI can assist with brainstorming, drafting, structural organization, and proofreading (Khalifa & Albadawy, 2024; Patel & Lam, 2023).
- The 6P pedagogical framework (Kong et al., 2024) offers a structured model for integrating AI into academic writing courses.

**Risks and concerns:**
- AI-generated texts tend to be grammatically polished but lack emotional nuance, rhetorical richness, and distinctive authorial voice (**Al Hosni, 2025**, thematic literature review in *AWEJ*).
- Hallucinated references remain a significant problem (Sanchez-Ramos et al., 2023).
- Over-reliance may erode students' independent writing skills and critical thinking.
- Detection of AI-generated text is unreliable, and detectors are biased against non-native English writers (Liang et al., 2023).

**Emerging policy landscape:**
- COPE (Committee on Publication Ethics) and ICMJE (International Committee of Medical Journal Editors) have issued positions requiring disclosure of AI use.
- Publisher policies are converging around permitting AI for language enhancement while prohibiting it for generating intellectual content, with the author retaining full responsibility.

**Lendvai (2025)**, in a scientometric analysis published in *SAGE*, mapped the rapidly growing literature on ChatGPT in academic writing (2022–2023), documenting the dual perception of the technology as both transformative and disruptive.

A particularly interesting finding: since 2023, there has been an increase in the use of "hyperbolic" language in scientific abstracts, potentially reflecting AI-assisted writing that amplifies claims to compete for attention.

## 9. Emerging Themes and Future Directions

Several threads are gaining momentum:

- **Plain language summaries**: Increasingly required by funders and journals (e.g., *eLife*, *PLOS*, and many Cochrane reviews), these demand that researchers develop parallel writing competencies.
- **Visual accessibility**: Epam et al. (2023), in *EPJ Data Science*, found that 2% of scientific figures are color-blind unsafe and 3% of diagnostic figures have low contrast, with surprising correlations to author h-index—more senior researchers produce less accessible figures.
- **Structured abstracts**: Evidence continues to accumulate that structured abstracts improve readability (Hartley, 2003, frequently cited), though adoption varies by field.
- **Writing across cultures**: The non-native English speaker perspective is increasingly centered, with recognition that the dominance of English in science creates structural inequities that AI may partially address.
- **Preprint culture**: The rise of preprints has shifted some writing norms, with less editorial gatekeeping of prose quality but more emphasis on rapid, clear communication.

## 11. Summary of Key Takeaways

For the working scientist seeking to improve their academic writing, the literature converges on several evidence-based principles:

1. **Readability matters for impact.** Papers that are easier to read are more likely to be cited, shared, and correctly interpreted. This is not merely aesthetic—it affects the reproducibility of science.

2. **Jargon is the primary enemy.** The single largest driver of declining readability is the proliferation of specialist vocabulary where common words would serve. Acronyms should be used sparingly and only when widely recognized.

3. **Narrative structure works.** Framing your paper as a story with characters, tension, and resolution (using frameworks like ABT or OCAR) improves engagement without sacrificing rigor.

4. **Active voice should be the default.** Use passive voice deliberately and strategically, not habitually.

5. **The curse of knowledge is real.** You are the least qualified person to judge whether your writing is clear, because you already understand it. Seek feedback from readers outside your subfield.

6. **Nominalizations kill prose.** Convert "zombie nouns" back into verbs: "We investigated" rather than "An investigation was conducted."

7. **AI tools can help, but with caveats.** They are useful for language polishing, brainstorming, and structural feedback, but they risk homogenizing voice and generating unreliable references. The author must remain the intellectual owner.

8. **Disciplinary conventions matter.** Style advice should be contextualized. What works in ecology may not work in mathematics. Read the best writers in your field and reverse-engineer their techniques.
