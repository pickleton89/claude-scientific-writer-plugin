**Foundational Principles**

Scientific writing is not merely a historical record of what you did in the laboratory; it is a persuasive argument designed to transfer complex information into the reader’s brain with minimal cognitive load \[Source: 1: writing\_principles.md\] . The defining characteristic of excellent scientific prose is clarity driven by a disinterested pursuit of truth \[Source: 2: Why-Academics-Stink-at-Writing-1-2.pdf\] . Good writing adopts a "classic style" where the writer acts as a guide, directing the reader’s gaze toward a shared reality \[Source: 2: Why-Academics-Stink-at-Writing-1-2.pdf\] . This contrasts sharply with the defensive, self-conscious style known as "academese," where authors obscure their meaning behind complex abstractions simply to prove their intellectual seriousness to their peers \[Source: 3: Why-Academics-Stink-at-Writing-1-2.pdf\] \[Source: 4: Why-Academics-Stink-at-Writing-1-2.pdf\] .

To achieve this clarity, you must overcome the "curse of knowledge"—the cognitive blind spot that makes it nearly impossible to remember what it is like not to know something you have already mastered \[Source: 5: Why-Academics-Stink-at-Writing-1-2.pdf\] \[Source: 6: Why-Academics-Stink-at-Writing-1-2.pdf\] . Because human brains evolved to process narratives, framing your research as a story is the most effective way to communicate data \[Source: 7: Science storytelling: Telling a story with data\] . A compelling scientific story features your object of study as the main character, the existing literature as the setting, the knowledge gap as the tension, and your findings as the resolution \[Source: 8: Science storytelling: Telling a story with data\] more\_horiz. Every word and paragraph must serve the reader, not the writer's ego.

**Argument Architecture**

The persuasive structure of a scientific document mirrors the scientific method itself, predominantly relying on the IMRAD format to build a coherent narrative arc \[Source: 11: imrad\_structure.md\] \[Source: 12: imrad\_structure.md\] . You should view the Introduction as a funnel that opens with a broad, accessible picture of the field, narrows down to the specific gap in current knowledge, and terminates with a clear statement of your exact hypothesis and approach \[Source: 13: imrad\_structure.md\] \[Source: 14: imrad\_structure.md\] . The Introduction is not an exhaustive literature review; it provides only the context necessary to establish tension \[Source: 15: imrad\_structure.md\] more\_horiz.

The Methods section serves a fundamentally different argumentative purpose: proving reproducibility. It must provide enough precise detail that an expert peer could perfectly replicate your work \[Source: 18: imrad\_structure.md\] \[Source: 19: imrad\_structure.md\] . The Results section then delivers the narrative thread of the data objectively. Here, your goal is to show the reader what happened without drifting into interpretation \[Source: 20: imrad\_structure.md\] \[Source: 21: imrad\_structure.md\] . Finally, the Discussion section resolves the narrative tension by answering the "So what?" and "What next?" questions \[Source: 22: imrad\_structure.md\] . A common failure pattern is using the Discussion merely to restate the results; instead, it must contextualize the findings within the broader literature, honestly address limitations, and articulate the theoretical or clinical implications \[Source: 23: imrad\_structure.md\] \[Source: 24: imrad\_structure.md\] . To construct this architecture efficiently, you should establish your tables and figures first, draft the Results and Methods, and save the Introduction, Discussion, and Abstract for last when the story's trajectory is fully clear \[Source: 25: Sainani SciWrite 5.1\] more\_horiz.

**Sentence and Paragraph Craft**

At the paragraph level, you must organize your thoughts so that each block of text contains exactly one main idea \[Source: 30: Sainani SciWrite 3.4\] \[Source: 31: Sainani SciWrite 3.4\] . Utilizing the Context-Content-Conclusion framework ensures that every paragraph orients the reader, delivers new evidence, and provides a clear takeaway \[Source: 32: writing\_principles.md\] . Furthermore, you should invert the typical scientific instinct to build up to a conclusion through endless details; instead, give the punchline early and follow it with the supporting evidence \[Source: 33: Sainani SciWrite 3.4\] \[Source: 34: Sainani SciWrite 3.4\] .

At the sentence level, the active voice is your primary tool for vigor and accountability. The English language relies on a Subject-Verb-Object progression, yet academics frequently invert this into the passive voice, omitting the actor entirely \[Source: 35: Sainani SciWrite 2.1\] more\_horiz. Passive constructions drain sentences of their energy and obscure responsibility. For example, instead of writing the weak passive sentence, "The activation of channels is induced by the depletion of stores," you should revise it to the active and direct, "Depleting stores activates channels" \[Source: 38: SKILL-example.md\] .

You must also actively manage technical density and eliminate rhetorical clutter. Cut throat-clearing introductory phrases like "It is important to note that" or "As it is well known," which dilute your claims \[Source: 39: SKILL-example.md\] . Hedging language exists on a spectrum; while science requires acknowledging uncertainty, over-hedging results in death-by-qualification \[Source: 40: Why-Academics-Stink-at-Writing-1-2.pdf\] \[Source: 41: Why-Academics-Stink-at-Writing-1-2.pdf\] . Instead of writing, "It could potentially possibly be argued that the policy might have some effect," you should boldly state exactly what the data supports: "The policy may affect outcomes" \[Source: 42: humanizer/SKILL.md at main · blader/humanizer · GitHub\] .

**Precision and Word Choice**

Precision in scientific writing requires selecting verbs that drive the action and avoiding the academic habit of nominalization—the process of embalming a strong verb into a lifeless "zombie noun" \[Source: 43: Why-Academics-Stink-at-Writing-1-2.pdf\] \[Source: 44: literature-survey-academic-writing.md\] . Nominalizations require you to pair a weak, generic verb with a heavy noun, thereby slowing the reader down \[Source: 45: Sainani SciWrite 1.2\] . For example, do not write that a study "provides a confirmation of" a hypothesis; simply write that the study "confirms" it. Revise "conduct an assessment of" to "assesses," and change "make an adjustment to" to "adjusts" \[Source: 46: SKILL-example.md\] .

While technical terminology is necessary, you must distinguish between precise jargon that serves as efficient shorthand for experts and pretentious jargon used merely to sound sophisticated \[Source: 47: Why-Academics-Stink-at-Writing-1-2.pdf\] more\_horiz. Define essential technical terms immediately upon their first use, and eliminate non-standard acronyms created solely for author convenience \[Source: 50: SKILL-example.md\] \[Source: 51: Why-Academics-Stink-at-Writing-1-2.pdf\] . Once you establish a term for a variable or group, you must adhere strictly to the "Banana Rule." Do not cycle through synonyms to avoid repetition; if your Methods section introduces an "obese group," your Results section must not suddenly refer to a "heavier group," as this forces the reader to wonder if a new variable has been introduced \[Source: 52: SKILL-example.md\] . Say exactly what the data supports using consistent, accurate language.

**Figures, Tables, and Text Integration**

Figures and tables are not mere illustrations; they are the primary evidence of your argument and often the first elements a peer reviewer will examine \[Source: 53: Sainani SciWrite 5.1\] . Consequently, they must pass the "Independence Test," meaning they must be entirely self-explanatory without requiring the reader to hunt through the main text for context \[Source: 54: figures\_tables.md\] \[Source: 55: figures\_tables.md\] . Every abbreviation, statistical symbol, and unit of measurement must be defined in the legend or footnote \[Source: 55: figures\_tables.md\] .

When integrating these visual elements with your prose, avoid redundant regurgitation. The main text should not serve as an audio-tour reading every data point in a table line-by-line \[Source: 56: Sainani SciWrite 5.2\] \[Source: 57: Sainani SciWrite 5.2\] . Instead, use the text to guide the reader's interpretation, highlight significant trends, and provide complementary comparisons. For example, rather than restating that the control group scored 30 and the treatment group scored 60, write that the treatment group doubled the performance of the control group \[Source: 58: Sainani SciWrite 5.2\] \[Source: 59: Sainani SciWrite 5.2\] . Maximize your data-ink ratio by removing three-dimensional effects, background gradients, and unnecessary gridlines that distract from the core scientific message \[Source: 60: figures\_tables.md\] .

**Abstracts, Titles, and First Impressions**

The title and abstract form the critical first impression of your work and dictate its discoverability \[Source: 61: Stapleton\_2022\_The\_ultimate\_academic\_writing\_toolkit\_.pdf\] . A title should be concise but highly descriptive, avoiding abbreviations and including the specific keywords that researchers will type into search engines \[Source: 61: Stapleton\_2022\_The\_ultimate\_academic\_writing\_toolkit\_.pdf\] more\_horiz.

The abstract is an exercise in the craft of compression, tasked with conveying the background, methodology, key quantitative results, and significance of the study in under 300 words \[Source: 64: imrad\_structure.md\] . Crucially, unless a target journal explicitly demands a structured abstract with bolded subheadings, you must write the abstract as a single, flowing, cohesive paragraph \[Source: 65: imrad\_structure.md\] . The abstract must be a completely standalone document. Never cite references within it, and ensure that any numbers presented perfectly match the data reported in the main text and tables—a common failure point that severely undermines author credibility \[Source: 66: Sainani SciWrite 4.6\] more\_horiz.

**Editing and Revision**

Great writing is not generated; it is rewritten. You must treat drafting and editing as strictly separate phases to avoid creative paralysis \[Source: 69: Sainani SciWrite 4.2\] more\_horiz. In your first draft, turn off your inner critic and focus entirely on getting your ideas down in a logical order \[Source: 72: Sainani SciWrite 4.4\] \[Source: 73: Sainani SciWrite 4.4\] . Elegance is achieved later, as professional writers spend the vast majority of their time in the revision phase \[Source: 74: Sainani SciWrite 4.2\] .

Apply a strict self-editing hierarchy: evaluate structure before paragraphs, paragraphs before sentences, and sentences before words \[Source: 75: Sainani SciWrite 4.5\] \[Source: 76: Sainani SciWrite 4.5\] . Begin with an organizational review, ensuring that similar ideas are grouped together and that arguments follow a coherent flow without jarring the reader back and forth \[Source: 77: Sainani SciWrite 4.3\] more\_horiz. Once the architecture is sound, refine your paragraphs for focus, and finally, audit your sentences for dead-weight phrases, buried verbs, and passive constructions \[Source: 80: Sainani SciWrite 4.5\] \[Source: 81: Sainani SciWrite 4.5\] . Reading your manuscript aloud is a highly effective diagnostic tool, as the ear will catch repetitive cadences, buried leads, and clumsy syntax that the eye glosses over \[Source: 82: Sainani SciWrite 4.5\] \[Source: 83: Sainani SciWrite 4.5\] .

**Principles in Tension**

Scientific writing is governed by competing conventions, and you must calibrate your approach based on the specific context.

The most prominent tension exists between the **active and passive voice**. While the active voice ("We collected the samples") is overwhelmingly prescribed for its clarity and vigor, the passive voice ("Samples were collected") remains a valid, and sometimes superior, convention in the Methods section. The passive voice correctly shifts the narrative emphasis away from the researchers—who are largely irrelevant to the protocol's mechanics—and places the focus squarely on the instruments, reagents, and procedures utilized \[Source: 84: Sainani SciWrite 5.3\] more\_horiz. You should default to the active voice in the Introduction, Results, and Discussion, but employ the passive voice in Methods when the action matters more than the actor.

A second major tension exists between **prescriptive conciseness and empirical discoverability**. Standard writing advice dictates ruthless brevity, urging authors to eliminate adjectives, simplify vocabulary, and shorten sentences \[Source: 88: Weinberger et al. - 2015 - Ten Simple (Empirical) Rules for Writing Science.pdf\] more\_horiz. However, empirical analyses of millions of published abstracts reveal a paradox: shorter, overly simplified abstracts actually receive *fewer* citations \[Source: 91: Weinberger et al. - 2015 - Ten Simple (Empirical) Rules for Writing Science.pdf\] . Writing abstracts with specific adjectives, adverbs, and robust technical detail results in higher citation impact, likely because comprehensive and terminology-rich prose performs vastly better in modern academic search engine algorithms \[Source: 91: Weinberger et al. - 2015 - Ten Simple (Empirical) Rules for Writing Science.pdf\] \[Source: 92: Weinberger et al. - 2015 - Ten Simple (Empirical) Rules for Writing Science.pdf\] . To resolve this, you must write the main body of your text with uncompromising conciseness for human readability, while allowing your title and abstract to remain dense with specific, descriptive keywords to optimize for machine discoverability.

### Model

An Agent Skill (`SKILL.md`) is an industry-standard Markdown file that provides AI models with reusable, domain-specific operating instructions \[Source: 1: README-skill-repo.md\] . Structuring this file correctly is what elevates an AI from a generic autocomplete engine into a rigorous editorial collaborator.

To build an effective `SKILL.md` for scientific writing, you must construct the file using a specific architecture: a YAML frontmatter header that dictates *when* the AI should activate the skill, followed by a structured Markdown body that dictates exactly *how* it should execute your standards \[Source: 1: README-skill-repo.md\] .

**1\. The YAML Frontmatter (The Trigger)** The document must begin with a YAML header enclosed in `---` dashes. This section does not teach the AI how to write; it teaches the system when to load your rules into its context window \[Source: 1: README-skill-repo.md\] . It should include:

-   **name** **and** **version**: Identifiers for the skill (e.g., `manuscript-writing-review`) \[Source: 2: SKILL-scientific-writer.md\] \[Source: 3: SKILL-example.md\] .-   **description** **or** **when\_to\_use**: A highly specific list of natural language triggers. Do not just write "Use for editing." Instead, list explicit user prompts such as "review my writing," "improve clarity," "edit for journal submission," or "fix passive voice" \[Source: 2: SKILL-scientific-writer.md\] \[Source: 3: SKILL-example.md\] .-   **allowed-tools**: Define what system capabilities the agent can access, such as reading, writing, or executing bash commands to parse files \[Source: 2: SKILL-scientific-writer.md\] .

**2\. Purpose and Absolute Constraints** Immediately following the YAML header, establish the AI's persona and its strict operational boundaries. You must explicitly forbid the AI from altering the underlying science. Define the role clearly: "You are an expert scientific writing reviewer tasked with transforming cluttered academic prose into precise communication" \[Source: 4: SKILL-example.md\] . Crucially, include a hard constraint section dictating that the AI must never alter scientific content, data, or technical claims \[Source: 5: README-skill-repo.md\] \[Source: 6: SKILL-example.md\] . Instruct it to preserve the author's voice and to respect disciplinary conventions, such as permitting the passive voice exclusively in the Methods section if appropriate \[Source: 6: SKILL-example.md\] .

**3\. Review Modes (User Interaction Framework)** Do not let the AI guess how you want to interact. Structure your guide to support distinct, named review modes so you can dictate the scope of the edit \[Source: 5: README-skill-repo.md\] \[Source: 7: SKILL-example.md\] .

-   **Full-Review**: Commands the AI to run all editorial checks on the entire document \[Source: 7: SKILL-example.md\] .-   **Section-Review**: Restricts the AI's focus to a single structural component, like the Introduction or Discussion \[Source: 7: SKILL-example.md\] .-   **Targeted**: Instructs the AI to run only one specific check, such as "fix passive voice" \[Source: 7: SKILL-example.md\] .-   **Interactive**: The most instructive mode, requiring the AI to walk you through the manuscript paragraph-by-paragraph, explaining every proposed change and waiting for your approval before proceeding \[Source: 7: SKILL-example.md\] \[Source: 8: HOW-TO-USE\_skill.md\] .

**4\. The Sequential Audit Passes (The Core Rules)** Organize your writing principles into a sequence of distinct, actionable audit passes. Instead of telling the AI to "be clear," command it to execute specific operations \[Source: 9: README-skill-repo.md\] :

-   **Pass 1: Clutter Extraction**: Instruct the AI to strip dead-weight introductory phrases (e.g., "It is important to note that") and eliminate redundant adjectives \[Source: 10: SKILL-example.md\] \[Source: 11: SKILL-example.md\] .-   **Pass 2: Voice and Verb Vitality**: Command the AI to hunt for "to-be" verbs paired with past participles (passive voice) and to resurrect "smothered verbs" by converting nominalizations like "conducts an assessment of" back to "assesses" \[Source: 12: SKILL-example.md\] .-   **Pass 3: Sentence Architecture**: Define testable metrics, such as commanding the AI to flag any sentence where more than 12 words separate the subject from the main verb (a "buried predicate") \[Source: 13: SKILL-example.md\] .-   **Pass 4: Keyword Consistency**: Explicitly enforce the "Banana Rule." Instruct the AI to extract key variables and terminology from the Methods section and flag any instance where a synonym is substituted later in the text \[Source: 14: SKILL-example.md\] .

**5\. The Blacklist and Anti-Patterns** This is often the highest-value section of the entire document \[Source: 15: 2026-03-19 AI Style Guides How to Help AI Write Like You.md\] . Left unchecked, AI models naturally generate sterile, predictable text laden with superficial markers of sophistication. Create a definitive blacklist of "AI-slop" patterns the model must eliminate on sight. Instruct the model to banish dramatic binary contrasts ("Not X, but Y"), to strip out generic promotional language ("groundbreaking," "vibrant"), and to remove fake profundity ("serving as a testament to") \[Source: 16: 2026-03-19 AI Style Guides How to Help AI Write Like You.md\] more\_horiz. Forbid the use of present participle phrases tacked onto the end of sentences (e.g., "underscoring its significance") which AI models use to simulate analytical depth \[Source: 19: humanizer/SKILL.md at main · blader/humanizer · GitHub\] .

**6\. Positive and Negative Examples** A rule paired with an example is vastly more effective for an AI than a rule alone \[Source: 20: 2026-03-19 AI Style Guides How to Help AI Write Like You.md\] . Do not simply tell the model to "avoid vague attributions"; show it a negative example ("Experts argue it plays a crucial role") paired with the expected humanized revision ("The river supports several endemic fish species, according to a 2019 survey") \[Source: 18: humanizer/SKILL.md at main · blader/humanizer · GitHub\] \[Source: 21: humanizer/SKILL.md at main · blader/humanizer · GitHub\] . Provide concrete before-and-after transformations so the model has a testable target for its output \[Source: 22: 2026-03-19 AI Style Guides How to Help AI Write Like You.md\] \[Source: 23: humanizer/SKILL.md at main · blader/humanizer · GitHub\] .

**7\. Output Format and Severity Tags** Finally, dictate exactly how the AI must present its findings. Never allow the AI to silently rewrite your text. Demand a structured report for every edit \[Source: 24: HOW-TO-USE\_skill.md\] \[Source: 25: SKILL-example.md\] . Instruct the AI to present the original text, the specific suggested revision, and the exact principle from the style guide that was violated \[Source: 24: HOW-TO-USE\_skill.md\] . Furthermore, force the AI to categorize its findings using a severity tag \[Source: 24: HOW-TO-USE\_skill.md\] :

-   **CRITICAL**: Edits that prevent the reader from being actively misled (e.g., inconsistent technical terminology or numerical mismatch between tables and text) \[Source: 26: SKILL-example.md\] .-   **MAJOR**: Edits that fix significant impairments to clarity, such as buried verbs or dense nominalizations \[Source: 26: SKILL-example.md\] .-   **MINOR**: Optional stylistic improvements to eliminate slight wordiness \[Source: 26: SKILL-example.md\] .

**The Paragraph as the Unit of Composition**

A paragraph should be treated as the fundamental unit of composition in your manuscript, engineered to contain exactly one main idea \[Source: 1: Sainani SciWrite 3.4\] more\_horiz. When authors force multiple concepts into a single block of text, they create a daunting wall of words that visually and cognitively overwhelms the reader \[Source: 2: Sainani SciWrite 3.4\] \[Source: 4: Sainani SciWrite 3.4\] . By keeping paragraphs relatively short—ideally between three and seven sentences—you naturally create essential white space on the page, which visually relieves the reader and makes the manuscript far more approachable \[Source: 3: writing\_principles.md\] \[Source: 5: Sainani SciWrite 3.4\] . If a paragraph runs longer than ten sentences, it usually indicates that the text needs to be split, whereas paragraphs shorter than two sentences often indicate an idea that requires further development or integration into another section \[Source: 3: writing\_principles.md\] . Furthermore, to maintain a coherent narrative arc, all paragraphs discussing a similar topic must be grouped together sequentially or collapsed into a single unit, rather than scattering related ideas across different sections of the manuscript \[Source: 6: Sainani SciWrite 4.3\] \[Source: 7: Sainani SciWrite 4.3\] .

**The Context-Content-Conclusion (C-C-C) Framework**

To ensure logical progression, every paragraph should be built upon the Context-Content-Conclusion (C-C-C) architectural framework \[Source: 8: Scientific Writing Standards Manual\] more\_horiz. The "Context" forms the opening sentence, which must immediately orient the reader by defining the topic or linking seamlessly to the idea presented in the preceding paragraph \[Source: 3: writing\_principles.md\] more\_horiz. Following this, the "Content" occupies the middle sentences to deliver the substance of the paragraph, presenting the new evidence, data, or technical analysis without unnecessary preamble \[Source: 3: writing\_principles.md\] more\_horiz.
